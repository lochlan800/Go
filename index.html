What is Go?

Go is one of the oldest board games in the world — invented in China over 2,500 years ago, and hugely popular across China, Japan, and Korea. It's known as Go (Japanese), Weiqi (Chinese), and Baduk (Korean).

The rules are astonishingly simple — you could learn them in five minutes — but the strategy is so deep that humans still discover new ideas after a lifetime of play. Players take turns placing stones on a grid, trying to surround more territory (empty space) than their opponent.

🎯 The one-sentence goal: Use your stones to surround and control more empty space than your opponent. Whoever controls more of the board wins.

The Board & The Stones

Go is played on a grid of lines. Stones are placed on the intersections (the points where lines cross) — not inside the squares like chess. The standard board is 19×19 lines, but beginners usually start on 9×9 or 13×13.

  • Black moves first, then players alternate.
  • Once placed, a stone never moves. It only leaves the board if it is captured.
  • You may place a stone on any empty intersection (with two small exceptions you'll learn next).

Liberties — the heartbeat of Go

Every stone breathes through its liberties: the empty points directly next to it (up, down, left, right — not diagonally).

A single black stone in the centre has 4 liberties (the four marked empty points). A stone on the edge has 3; in the corner, only 2.

🫁 When a stone (or a connected group of stones) has its last liberty filled by the enemy, it is captured and removed from the board. That's the whole engine of the game.

Stones of the same colour that touch side-by-side form a group and share their liberties — they live or die together. Connecting your stones makes them stronger.

The two special rules

1. Suicide is not allowed

You may not place a stone where it would have no liberties — unless that same move captures enemy stones (which then frees up space). The board in this app enforces this automatically.

2. The Ko rule (no instant repeats)

You cannot make a move that recreates the exact same board position as your previous turn. This prevents an endless loop where two players capture the same single stone back and forth forever. You'll see this called out as "Ko" when you play.

✅ That's genuinely it. Place stones, surround to capture, don't suicide, don't repeat. Everything else is strategy.

Capturing stones

To capture, you fill in all the liberties of an enemy stone or group. The moment the last liberty is gone, those stones are lifted off the board and become your prisoners.

This white stone has just one liberty left (marked ✕). It's in atari — one move from capture.

Black fills that last point — white is captured and removed.

⚠️ "Atari" is the most important word for beginners. It means a stone or group has only one liberty left and can be captured next turn. When you're in atari, you usually want to either run (add a stone to gain liberties) or connect to a friendly group — or accept the loss if it's small.

Why you can't just be captured everywhere: eyes & life

A group of stones is alive forever (uncapturable) if it surrounds two separate empty points inside itself. These internal empty points are called eyes.

This black group has two eyes (the two inner empty points). White can never fill both at once — filling one would be suicide — so this group can never be captured. It is alive.

👁️👁️ The golden rule of survival: a group with two real eyes is permanently alive. Making your groups live (and your opponent's die) is the central battle of every game.

How the game ends

The game doesn't run until the board is full. It ends when both players pass in a row — meaning neither sees a useful move left. Then you count the score.

You'd pass when the borders between black and white are settled and playing more stones would only fill your own territory (which loses you points).

Counting the score

Your score is your territory plus (depending on scoring style) your captures:

  • Territory = empty points completely surrounded by only your colour.
  • Captures / prisoners = enemy stones you removed.

This app uses simple area scoring: your score = (your stones on the board) + (empty points surrounded only by you). Highest score wins.

Black surrounds the empty points on the left; white surrounds the right. Each empty region counts for whoever walls it off. Points touching both colours are neutral (no one's).

⚖️ Komi: because Black moves first (an advantage), White is given a few bonus points to compensate — typically 6.5. The half-point also guarantees there's never a tie. This app applies komi for you.

So how do I actually win?

Control more of the board than your opponent. You do that by:

  • Sketching out large frameworks of territory in the opening.
  • Keeping your own groups alive (two eyes!).
  • Reducing or invading your opponent's areas.
  • Capturing weak enemy groups when you can.

You almost never need to capture everything. Winning by even half a point is a win. Go rewards balance and patience over pure aggression.

Beginner tips that instantly help

1Corners → Sides → Centre. Territory is easiest to build in the corners (the edges do part of the walling for you), harder on the sides, and hardest in the wide-open centre. Strong players grab corners first.

2Don't play too close together. Beginners cram stones in a tight clump. Spread out a little — each stone should be doing new work. But don't spread so thin you can be cut apart.

3Stay connected. Connected groups are strong and hard to kill. Isolated single stones are easy targets.

4Make two eyes to live. If a group can't make two eyes, it will eventually die. Always know whether your groups are alive.

5Watch for atari. If your stone has one liberty left, you're about to lose it. And look for chances to put the opponent in atari.

6Sometimes sacrifice. Losing a stone or two to gain a bigger position is good play. Don't desperately save everything.

7It's okay to pass. When there's nothing useful left, pass. Filling your own territory loses points.

🌱 The fastest way to improve: play lots of quick 9×9 games. Don't worry about playing "correctly" — just get a feel for capturing, connecting, and making eyes. Understanding follows experience.
